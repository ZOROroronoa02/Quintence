Most data projects don't fail at the technology. They fail at the seam between the people who build the pipeline and the people who ship the product.

## The pattern

It usually goes like this. The pipeline team finishes: warehouse modeled, dashboards delivered, docs written. Then the product team starts ~ and discovers the pipeline answers slightly different questions than the product needs. Timestamps mean different things. IDs don't join. Freshness is "daily" when the feature needs "hourly."

Nobody did bad work. The work was just done on opposite sides of a wall, and context leaked through every handoff. By the time the product ships, it has quietly rebuilt half the pipeline's logic in application code ~ untested, undocumented, and drifting.

## Three symptoms

**Duplicated metric logic.** The same revenue definition lives in dbt and in a product service, written months apart by people who never met. They agree until the day they don't.

**The dashboard number ≠ the app number.** Leadership sees one figure on Monday; customers see another in the product. Both are "correct" under different definitions nobody wrote down.

**Nobody owns freshness.** The pipeline team monitors job success. The product team assumes data is fresh. Neither watches the actual question: is the number on this screen current?

## What one owner looks like

One team holding schema, pipeline, and UI changes the physics. The schema becomes a contract both sides code against. Metric definitions live in dbt and are imported ~ not reimplemented ~ by product code. And one team is on call for the whole path, so a freshness regression pages someone who can fix it end to end.

This is the entire reason Quintence is shaped the way it is: a small senior team that refuses to hand work to itself across a wall.

## When teams can't merge

Sometimes you can't merge the teams ~ and that's fine, as long as you merge the contract. Three practices carry most of the weight: definition tests that assert pipeline outputs match product expectations, joint reviews where both sides look at the same numbers before launch, and a short contract doc that states freshness, grain, and ownership for every shared dataset.

Walls are organizational facts. Leaks are a choice.
